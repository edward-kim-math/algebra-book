    Subsection Applications

    Exponents appear in a variety of applications. Here are some common scenarios where exponent rules are essential:
    1. Compound Interest (Finance)
      🔗
      Scenario: You deposit money in a bank account that compounds interest.
      🔗
      Formula: \(A = P(1+r)^t\) or \(A = Pe^{rt}\) for continuous compounding.
      🔗
      Exponent Rule Used: \((a^x)^y = a^{xy}\)
      🔗
      Example: If $1000 is invested at 5% per year for 3 years: \(A = 1000(1.05)^3\text{.}\) Using exponent rules: \((1.05)^3 = (1.05 \cdot 1.05) \cdot 1.05 = 1.157625\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Example: Given \(P = 1000\text{,}\) \(r = 0.04\text{,}\) and \(t = 5\text{,}\) simplify the value of \(Pe^{rt}\text{.}\) This is a finance problem, and the value answers the following question: if $1000 is deposited now in an account earning 4% interest compounded continuously, what will the account balance be in 5 years?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Population Growth / Decay (Biology and Chemistry)
      🔗
      Scenario: Populations, bacteria, or radioactive isotopes grow or decay exponentially.
      🔗
      Formula: \(P(t) = P_0 \cdot r^t\) or \(N(t) = N_0 e^{-kt}\)
      🔗
      Exponent Rules Used: \((a^x)^y = a^{xy}\text{,}\) \(a^x \cdot a^y = a^{x+y}\)
      🔗
      Example: A bacteria culture doubles every 3 hours: \(N(t) = 1000 \cdot 2^{t/3}\text{.}\) After 6 hours: \(2^{6/3} = 2^2 = 4\) times the initial population.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Physics: Scaling Laws
      🔗
      Scenario: Volume, area, or mass often scale with powers of linear dimensions.
      🔗
      Exponent Rules Used: \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\text{,}\) \((a/b)^n = a^n / b^n\)
      🔗
      Example: Volume of a cube with side \(s\text{:}\) \(V = s^3\text{.}\) If the side doubles: \((2s)^3 = 2^3 \cdot s^3 = 8 s^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Computer Science: Algorithms and Complexity
      🔗
      Scenario: Exponential growth in possibilities.
      🔗
      Example: A 4-character password with 26 letters: \(26^4\) total possibilities.
      🔗
      Exponent Rule: Multiply powers of the same base: \(26^4 = 26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Chemistry: Solution Dilution
      🔗
      Scenario: Concentration decreases by a fixed ratio with each dilution.
      🔗
      Formula: After n dilutions: \(C_n = C_0 (1/2)^n\)
      🔗
      Exponent Rule: \((a^x)^y = a^{xy}\) to simplify repeated dilutions.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Physics: Sound and Light Intensity
      🔗
      Scenario: Intensity decreases with the square of the distance.
      🔗
      Formula: \(I = P / r^2\)
      🔗
      Exponent Rule: \((a/b)^n = a^n / b^n\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Engineering: Material Strength
      🔗
      Scenario: Strength often scales as a power of thickness.
      🔗
      Formula: \(Strength \propto t^3\)
      🔗
      Exponent Rules: \((a^x)^y = a^{xy}\) or \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n b^n\) to compute effects of scaling dimensions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Exponents

    Writing expressions involving exponents provides a powerful way to describe certain quantifiable phenomena in a variety of applications. We will need some exponent rules that help us rewrite expressions involving exponents. These rules will also help us simplify expressions involving exponents. Before introducing the rules, let’s make sure we’re on the same page about what an exponent means.
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.1. What does an exponent mean?

    Exponentiation can be interpreted to mean repeated multiplication. An exponent is a way to represent repeated multiplication of a number by itself: \(a^n\) means \(a\) is multiplied by itself a total of \(n\) times, where \(n\) is a positive integer.
    🔗
    🔗
    This principle only really makes sense when \(n\) is a positive integer such as \(1, 2, 3, ...\text{.}\) For example, \(a^4\) means \(a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a\text{,}\) and \(a^6\) means \(a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a\text{.}\) We will later talk about situations where the exponent is zero, a negative number, or a fraction. In those stranger situations, Principle 1.5.1 doesn’t apply.
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.2. Exponent rules, part 1.

    • \(\displaystyle a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Reading from left to right, the first formula says that when multiplying two expressions with the same base, we can add the exponents (in which case we write the base once). This is why the left side has \(a^m a^n\) where the same base is \(a\) appears twice, while the right side has \(a^{m+n}\) where the base \(a\) appears only once. The first formula’s requirement requiring that the two expressions being multiplied together have the same base is analogous to the requirement that two fractions being added or subtracted must have the same denominator. Reading from right to left, an expression of the form \(a^{m+n}\) can be rewritten as \(a^m a^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Reading from left to right, the second formula says that when taking an exponential expression such as \(a^m\) and raise it to a power, we can multiply the exponents. Read from right to left, an expression of the form \(a^{mn}\) can be rewritten as \((a^m)^n\text{,}\) meaning that when we see an expression with an exponent that is a product, we can rewrite it as an expression where the base is raised to one of the factors, and then the entire expression wrapped in parentheses is raised to the other factor.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.3.

    Simplify \((a^3)^5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\text{,}\) we have \((a^3)^5 = a^{3 \cdot 5} = a^{15}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.4.

    Simplify \(a^3 a^5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{,}\) we have \(a^3 a^5 = a^{3+5} = a^8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.5.

    Simplify \(2(x^4)^2 + 5x^4x^2 + 10x^6 + 20x^8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\text{,}\) we have \((x^4)^2 = x^{4 \cdot 2} = x^8\text{.}\) Thus, \(2(x^4)^2 = 2x^8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, using the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{,}\) we have \(5x^4x^2 = 5x^{4+2} = 5x^6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The third term and fourth term are already simplified: \(10x^6\) and \(20x^8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Finally, using the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\) again, we have \(20x^8 = 20x^8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now we can combine like terms: \(2x^8 + 5x^6 + 10x^6 + 20x^8 = (2 + 20)x^8 + (5 + 10)x^6 = 22x^8 + 15x^6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    An example like the one we just did is meant to provide a bit of caution. After applying exponent rules, we get to the expression \(2x^8 + 5x^6 + 10x^6 + 20x^8\text{.}\) It is tempting to say that the final expression is \(37x^{14}\text{.}\) Combine only what we can (which is just up to like terms).
    🔗

    Example 1.5.6.

    Simplify \(c^7 c^{1/2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{,}\) we have \(c^7 c^{1/2} = c^{7 + 1/2} = c^{14/2 + 1/2} = c^{15/2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In a simpler example, namely Example 1.5.4, we wrote \(a^3 a^5 = a^{3+5} = a^8\text{,}\) though many prefer to skip the “middle step” and directly go from \(a^3 a^5\) to \(a^8\text{.}\) This is fine to do when we can do the simplification in our heads, but in the example we just did, it might be harder to see how the sum of the exponents simplifies. That’s why even in the easier problem, we wrote out the step. In a situation like this that’s more complicated, it is practical to write the addition (without having simplified yet), and then in the next step(s), we can “zoom in” focusing only in the exponent area, and simplify the exponent from \(7+\frac{1}{2}\) to \(\frac{14}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\) to \(\frac{15}{2}\text{.}\) Even if you see it in this example without writing all the steps, there’s definitely eventually going to be a challenging enough example where we can’t just picture it all. Being willing to write that two things are added together without immediately simplifying it is a power tool to have.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.7.

    Simplify \(m^4 m^{\frac{3}{10}} m^{\frac{2}{5}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \amp m^4 m^{\frac{3}{10}} m^{\frac{2}{5}}\\ \amp= m^{4 + \frac{3}{10} + \frac{2}{5}}\\ \amp= m^{\frac{40}{10} + \frac{3}{10} + \frac{4}{10}}\\ \amp= m^{\frac{47}{10}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.8.

    Simplify \(m^4 (m^{\frac{3}{10}})^{\frac{2}{5}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \amp m^4 (m^{\frac{3}{10}})^{\frac{2}{5}}\\ \amp= m^{4} m^{\frac{3}{10} \cdot \frac{2}{5}}\\ \amp= m^4 m^{\frac{6}{50}}\\ \amp= m^{4 + \frac{6}{50}}\\ \amp= m^{\frac{200}{50} + \frac{6}{50}}\\ \amp= m^{\frac{206}{50}} \end{align*}
    Unlike the previous question, notice that we had to use both exponent rules in Principle 1.5.2 to simplify this expression.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We showed two standard formulas in Principle 1.5.2 how they usually look: \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\) and \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\text{.}\) However, these two are often confused for each other, and it’s easier to see why people confuse these two formulas when we see all operations written in, with none hidden:
    1. \(\displaystyle a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle (a^m)^{n} = a^{m \cdot n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    These are actually the same formulas, and I admit they look bulkier with all the operations explicitly shown. But this helps us see why there is confusion! Notice that the formula that has multiplication on the left has addition on the right, while the second formula has multiplication the right. So, it is incorrect to turn \(c^2 \cdot c^3\) into \(c^{2 \cdot 3}\) which would eventually be \(c^6\text{,}\) but I can understand why people would make that error! (Instead, the correct simplification is \(c^2 \cdot c^3 = c^{2+3} = c^5\text{.}\))
    🔗
    This leads us to ask a more general question: how are we supposed to know what to do when we see an expression of the form \(a^m \cdot a^n\) versus what we are supposed to do when we see an expression of the form \((a^m)^n\text{?}\) The short answer (just memorize the formulas) is really unsatisfying. It’s easy to “cross the wires” here. Fortunately, there is a tool that we have that can help us remember how each of these formulas end, and more importantly, if we keep practicing the process that we’re about to suggest, it actually provides a reason why these formulas are true in the first place! That’s a win-win!
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.9. How to keep track of exponent formulas.

    For the two formulas we presented in Principle 1.5.2, the left sides said \(a^m \cdot a^n\) and \((a^m)^n\text{.}\) How can know what is on the right side of each formula? What can we do when we’ve forgotten what’s on the right side of each formula? And finally, how can we know why the right sides that get presented are actually legitimate in the first place? The answer to all of these questions is to “expand” the expression after selecting your own constants for the exponents (but not for the base), and expand based on Principle 1.5.1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.10.

    Suppose we recall that there is a formula that starts with \(a^m \cdot a^n\text{,}\) but we can’t remember what is on the right side of the formula. Say we also recall there is a formula that starts with \((a^m)^n\text{,}\) but we can’t remember what is on the right side of that formula either. Which of these two expressions involves adding the exponents, and which involes multiplying the exponents? That is, which has a power of \(m+n\) and which has a power of \(m \cdot n\text{?}\) To figure this out, we can pick our own values for \(m\) and \(n\text{,}\) and then expand each expression based on Principle 1.5.1. While we can pick \(m\) and \(n\) to be as large as we want, let’s pick small values to make the expansion easier. That said, we can’t pick \(m\) and \(n\) to be too small: for example if we picked both \(m=2\) and \(n=2\text{,}\) both \(m+n\) and \(m \cdot n\) would be equal to \(4\text{,}\) which wouldn’t help us distinguish between the two formulas.
    🔗
    Let’s pick \(m=2\) and \(n=3\text{,}\) which is about as minimal as we can pick. Based on what exponents mean in Principle 1.5.1:
    1. We can expand \(a^m \cdot a^n\) as \(a^2 \cdot a^3 = (a \cdot a) \cdot (a \cdot a \cdot a) = a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a = a^5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We can expand \((a^m)^n\) as \((a^2)^3 = (a \cdot a)^3 = (a \cdot a) \cdot (a \cdot a) \cdot (a \cdot a) = a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a = a^6\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Noting that \(5 = 2 + 3\) and \(6 = 2 \cdot 3\text{,}\) we see that \(a^m \cdot a^n\) involves adding the exponents, while \((a^m)^n\) involves multiplying the exponents.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.5.11.

    Note that we never picked a specific value for \(a\text{.}\) It’s not that we can’t. We can! But, if we did (say we picked \(a=5\)), then we’d be looking at an expression like \(5^2 \cdot 5^3\text{,}\) and with constants in all places, it’s too tempting to work on simplyfing arithmetic, which is both time-consuming, and hides what it is we’re trying to get out of this. The point is that we just want to count how many times the base \(a\) appears as a factor, and record that number of appearances as an exponent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.12.

    Say we need to simplify \(x^\frac{1}{3} x^\frac{2}{5}\text{,}\) but forgot how the formulas go. Go through a process to recover the formula, then apply it it to simplify this expression.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Suppose we forgot how the formulas go. The shape of what we have is multiplying to expressions that have the same base: this is addressed by \(a^m \cdot a^n\text{.}\) The question becomes whether we add or multiply the exponents. To figure this out, we can work on the side where we temporarily pick our own values for \(m\) and \(n\text{.}\) Let’s say we pick \(m=4\) and \(n=3\text{,}\) which are relatively small numbers. Then, based on Principle 1.5.1, we can expand \(a^m \cdot a^n\) as \(a^4 \cdot a^3 = (a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a) \cdot (a \cdot a \cdot a) = a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a\) and counting how many times the base is a factor, this is \(a^7\text{.}\) So, in the case of \(a^m \cdot a^n\text{,}\) we add the exponents, which is why the full formula (which we might forget, but we just recovered through this process) is \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now we can apply this formula: \(x^\frac{1}{3} x^\frac{2}{5} = x^{\frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{5}} = x^{\frac{5}{15} + \frac{6}{15}} = x^{\frac{11}{15}}\text{.}\) Because it is challenging to simplify the addition of these fractions right away, we just first wrote the addition without simplifying it by writing \(x^{\frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{5}}\text{,}\) though we still needed to write \(x\) as the base in order to ensure that the equal sign truly means equal. In the next step, we focused on simplifying the exponent area (by mentally zooming in) and found a common denominator.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The natural question arises: it’s easy to remember what to do to get these formulas when the author or teacher hints to me what to do, but how am I supposed to recall? In the end, the two formulas we introduced (and several more!) all can be recovered using the same tool: select values for exponents (but not for the base), and expand based on asking yourself what exponents mean (Principle 1.5.1), then count the number of times the base appears. This works for the two formulas we introduced, and it also works for several more formulas that we will introduce next. Before showing the new formulas, it would be good to put this useful tool into a box!
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.13.

    Based on what exponents mean in Principle 1.5.1
    • Select values for exponents. (To avoid the temptation to do unnecessary arithmetic, do not select a value for base(s).)
      🔗
      Expand based on what exponents mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Count the number of times the base appears, and rewrite based on what exponents mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s introduce a couple new formulas where the tool in Principle 1.5.13 applies. Let’s then demonstrate full examples of how to practice this tool, then do full examples of computations based on the new formulas.
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.14. Exponent rules, part 2.

    • \(\displaystyle (a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    It would be easy to fall into the temptation of saying “Well, if I don’t remember what’s on the other side of one of these exponent formulas, I can just look it up.” Let me be the first to admit that, yes, of course you can look it up in certain situations. However, as this algebra course progresses, we will be looking at more involved problems, and in these problems, applyning exponent rules will need to happen as one pieces of the process. It will really slow us down if we don’t gain a little bit of familiarity with these formulas. (Imagine if a professional chef who has to keep inventing new recipes works from the assumption of saying “I don’t want to spend the brain space to think about the differences of the taste of basil, cinnamon, rosemary, and anise.” Sure, anyone -- including the chef -- can go online and find descriptions of all four of these spices. But intentionally lacking familiarity is going to slow this cook down.) Just like in speaking a foreign language, learning these exponent formulas is going to take some practice through repetition. It won’t stick just looking at them once or studying them once. Before we dig into what’s next, in the hopes that you’ll really read what’s next with effort, I also just want to say that it’s hard for anyone to memorize a thing when there’s no meaning behind it. It would be hard to go in a room full of thirty strangers and memorize each person’s name, address, and phone number if each person told you their info once. The reason is that all the data is just disconnected: the phone number you learn for a stranger will just be a bunch of digits. These exponent formulas can seem the same way if there’s no meaning attached to it. And people try doing that! People try to memorize these formulas, and struggle to do so. Instead, let’s practice a technique that helps us memorize these formulas better each time we practice what we’re about to do, and more importantly, it does the job not only by having us stare (like we often passively do with flashcards), but instead shows us the meaning behind the notation which convinces us why the formula is true in the first place!
    🔗

    Example 1.5.15.

    Suppose that in the course of working on an algebra problem, we encounter the expression \(\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^5\text{.}\) Based on the shape of that expression, we think about a formula where one side says \(\left({a}{b}\right)^n\text{.}\) If we have forgotten the other side of that formula, how do we recover it? Then, how do we apply the recovered formula to our expression?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The original expression \(\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^5\) is in the format of \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n\) because the original expression is a fraction (numerator of denominator) raised to a power.
    🔗
    We want to figure out what goes on the other side of the formula that has \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n\) on one side. To apply Principle 1.5.13, we should only pick values for exponent(s), but not for base(s), so we will select something for \(n\) but not for \(a\) or \(b\text{.}\) Let’s pick \(n=3\text{,}\) which is a small positive integer. Then, Principle 1.5.1 reminds us what an exponent means, so we can expand \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n\) as
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^3 = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \cdot a \cdot a}{b \cdot b \cdot b} = \frac{a^3}{b^3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, we have recovered the formula
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}. \end{equation*}
    We can apply this formula to \(\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^5\) and we see that
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^5 = \frac{c^5}{2^5}. \end{equation*}
    We just illustrated using the formula, but because the denominator has no variables, we can simplify slightly, getting \(\frac{c^5}{32}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Stick with it! This takes practice. You may never have had to do this in algebra before, but I encourage you to try this. This kind of activity actually gives us a real reason to believe the formulas are true in the first place, and it is a lot easier to remember and know a thing that you believe is true with evidence, rather than a thing that someone just told you to believe is true.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.16.

    A major algebra formula has \((a \cdot b)^n\) on one side. How can we recover what goes on the other side, and in doing so, help reveal why the formula is true in the first place?
    🔗
    Solution.
    To apply Principle 1.5.13, we should only pick values for exponent(s), but not for base(s), so we will select something for \(n\) but not for \(a\) or \(b\text{.}\) Let’s pick \(n=4\text{,}\) which is a small positive integer. Then, Principle 1.5.1 reminds us what an exponent means, so we can expand \((a \cdot b)^n\) as
    \begin{equation*} (a \cdot b)^4 = (a \cdot b) \cdot (a \cdot b) \cdot (a \cdot b) \cdot (a \cdot b) = a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot b \cdot b \cdot b \cdot b = a^4 b^4\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, we have recovered the formula
    \begin{equation*} (a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n. \end{equation*}

    Example 1.5.18.

    Simplify \((gh^2)^5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\text{,}\) we have \((gh^2)^5 = (g)^5 (h^2)^5\text{.}\) Note, in our use of this exponent rule, we treated \(g\) as \(a\) and \(h^2\) as \(b\text{,}\) since this assignment of values truly captures the idea that the value of \(a\) and the value of \(b\) are multiplied together, and that result is taken to the power \(n\text{.}\) When we wrote \((g)^5 (h^2)^5\) we could have skipped the parentheses around the \(g\) and write \(g^5(h^2)^5\) instead. However, we kept parentheses around \(h^2\text{.}\) Otherwise, there’s too much going on, since in the next step, we use \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\) to turn \((h^2)^5\) into \(h^{2 \cdot 5}\text{.}\) It doesn’t make sense for us to skip writing the work the very first time we ever need to combine the use of \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) and the the use of \((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\text{.}\) Then, using the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\text{,}\) we have \((h^2)^5 = h^{2 \cdot 5} = h^{10}\text{.}\) Based on the discussion above, a thorough answer would be:
    \begin{equation*} (gh^2)^5 = g^5(h^2)^5 = g^5 h^{2 \cdot 5} = g^5 h^{10} \end{equation*}
    while an answwer (that is still based on the discussion above) that skips a little bit more would be:
    \begin{equation*} (gh^2)^5 = g^5h^{10}. \end{equation*}

Then, we use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite each factor \((x^2)^4 = x^{2 \cdot 4} = x^8\) and also to rewrite \((y^3)^4 = y^{3 \cdot 4} = y^{12}\text{.}\) Showing all of what was explained above, writing with good notation (see where the equal signs are), we have:

    Example 1.5.21.

    Simplify \((xy^4)^3 x^8 (x^3 z)^{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\text{,}\) we have \((xy^4)^3 = x^3 (y^4)^3 = x^3 y^{12}\text{.}\) Please note that inside the first parentheses where the text is \(xy^4\text{,}\) we must read this using the Order of Operations, and recognize that the exponent \(4\) applies only to \(y\text{,}\) and not to \(x\text{.}\) Thus, \((xy^4)^3 x^8 (x^3 z)^{10} = (x^3 y^{12}) x^8 (x^3 z)^{10}\text{.}\) Next, using the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) again, we have \((x^3 z)^{10} = (x^3)^{10} z^{10} = x^{30} z^{10}\text{.}\) Thus, \((xy^4)^3 x^8 (x^3 z)^{10} = (x^3 y^{12}) x^8 (x^{30} z^{10})\text{.}\) Now, using the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} x^3 x^8 x^{30} = x^{3+8+30} = x^{41}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \((xy^4)^3 x^8 (x^3 z)^{10} = x^{41} y^{12} z^{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In Principle 1.5.2, we saw \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{,}\) which in symbols says that multiplying two expressions that have the same base can be rewrtten as a single expression with that same base, with the exponent being the sum of the two starting exponents. Now, we show a formula for dividing two expressions that have the same base, and we see on the other sid eof the formula that we subtract the two starting exponents:
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.22.

    \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.23.

    The algebra formula we just presented has \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) on one side. How can we recover what goes on the other side, and in doing so, help reveal why the formula is true in the first place?
    🔗
    Solution.
    To apply Principle 1.5.13, we should only pick values for exponent(s), but not for base(s), so we will select something for \(m\) and \(n\) but not for \(a\text{.}\) Let’s pick \(m=5\) and \(n=2\text{.}\) Then, Principle 1.5.1 reminds us what an exponent means, so we can expand \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a^5}{a^2} = \frac{a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a}{a \cdot a}. \end{equation*}
    Then, we can cancel two factors of \(a\) from the numerator and denominator, getting \(\frac{a \cdot a \cdot a}{1} = a^3\text{.}\) We see that in the example where \(m=5\) and \(n=2\text{,}\) this simplified to an expression where, with the base \(a\) written once, the exponent is \(5-2\text{.}\) Therefore, we have recovered the formula
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.24.

    Simplify \(\frac{c^7}{c^3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    While we can use our new formula in Principle 1.5.22, we can skip this formula and instead apply Principle 1.5.1. Based on Principle 1.5.1, we can expand \(\frac{c^7}{c^3} = \frac{c \cdot c \cdot c \cdot c \cdot c \cdot c \cdot c}{c \cdot c \cdot c}\text{.}\) Then, we can cancel three factors of \(c\) from the numerator and denominator, getting \(\frac{c \cdot c \cdot c \cdot c}{1} = c^4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Using the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) in Principle 1.5.22, we have \(\frac{c^7}{c^3} = c^{7-3} = c^4\text{,}\) which leads to the same final answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We found two ways to answer the last example. One answer used the formula given in Principle 1.5.22, while the other answer didn’t (and only used what exponents mean from Principle 1.5.1). We bring this up for several reasons:
    • One natural reaction to hearing “we didn’t need the formula” in Principle 1.5.22 is to ask why bother showing this formula in the first place. In a small enough example, it’s fine to expand based on Principle 1.5.1, but if we had a larger example like
      \begin{equation*} \frac{u^{30000}}{u^{41}} \end{equation*}
      it would be impractical to expand based on Principle 1.5.1, since nobody wants to write out \(u\) as a factor 30,000 times in the numerator and 41 times in the denominator. In this case, the formula in Principle 1.5.22 is very useful, which lets us quickly write
      \begin{equation*} \frac{u^{30000}}{u^{41}} = u^{30000-41} = u^{29959}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • It is also good to see that we got the same final answer of \(c^4\) both using the new formula and not using the new formula. Getting the same final answer through two different routes (an old route and a new route) gives us more confidence that the new formula is correct.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Finally, it’s just good to keep in mind that there are often multiple ways to answer a question. This is true in math, as it is in life. It is good to be flexible and adaptable. There is often more than one way to approach a problem. Especially in the area of exponents (and radicals, which we will see next), there are often multiple ways to answer a question. I bring this up because in several of the practice problems that are to follow, I will be providing several different ways to answer the same question. In fact, for many of the questions we’ll see, there are many possible different first steps! This is not to confuse you, but rather to show you that there are often multiple ways to approach a problem. Moreover, if a question naturally has several different answers (with different starts) that are all equally reasonable as answers (none much longer than the other), then I think it does a disservice to you to not show multiple answers: this might lead you to think that the way you’re thinking about the problem doesn’t work. My experience working with students has shown me that, especially in the area of exponents and radicals, multiple students start the same problem differently with approaches that all work!
      🔗

    Example 1.5.25.

    Simplify \(\frac{u^{2/3}}{u^{1/6}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since we are dividing two exponential expressions that have the same base, the formula \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) in Principle 1.5.22 applies.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{u^{2/3}}{u^{1/6}} = u^{\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{6}} = u^{\frac{4}{6} - \frac{1}{6}} = u^{\frac{3}{6}} = u^{\frac{1}{2}}. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the second step, we found a common denominator of 6 to subtract the two fractions. In the last step, we simplified the fraction \(\frac{3}{6}\text{.}\) It was very useful to write the step \(u^{\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{6}}\) even though we didn’t simplify the exponent right away: we worked on getting a common denominator for the subtraction of fractions in the step after that. I can understand when someone doesn’t do this in an easier problem like \(\frac{u^7}{u^2}\text{,}\) skipping the step of writing \(u^{7-2}\) and going directly to \(u^5\text{,}\) but that’s because it’s easier to simplify \(7-2\) than it is to simplify \(\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{6}\) in your head. When the simplification of the exponent is not immediate, it’s just nicer to take it a little slower and write what the subtraction is first before simplifying the subtraction.

    Example 1.5.28.

    Simplify \(\left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    First, we will zoom in to the portion of the expression that says \((x^4y^5)^2\text{,}\) where we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^4y^5)^2\) as \((x^4)^2 (y^5)^2 = x^{4 \cdot 2} y^{5 \cdot 2} = x^8 y^{10}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10} = \left(\frac{x^8 y^{10}}{x^2 y^3}\right)^{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, we use the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) to rewrite \(\frac{x^8}{x^2} = x^{8-2} = x^6\text{,}\) and also to rewrite \(\frac{y^{10}}{y^3} = y^{10-3} = y^7\text{.}\) Thus, \(\left(\frac{x^8 y^{10}}{x^2 y^3}\right)^{10} = (x^6 y^7)^{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Finally, we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^6 y^7)^{10} = (x^6)^{10} (y^7)^{10}\text{,}\) and then use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \((x^6)^{10} = x^{6 \cdot 10} = x^{60}\) and also to rewrite \((y^7)^{10} = y^{7 \cdot 10} = y^{70}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \((x^6 y^7)^{10} = x^{60} y^{70}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In summary,
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10} = \left(\frac{x^8 y^{10}}{x^2 y^3}\right)^{10} = (x^6 y^7)^{10} = (x^6)^{10} (y^7)^{10} = x^{6 \cdot 10} y^{7 \cdot 10} = x^{60} y^{70}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    We could have started by using \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\) to rewrite \(\left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10} = \frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then, we use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \(((x^4y^5)^2)^{10} = (x^4y^5)^{2 \cdot 10} = (x^4y^5)^{20}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^4y^5)^{20}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^4y^5)^{20} = (x^4)^{20} (y^5)^{20}\text{,}\) and then use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \((x^4)^{20} = x^{4 \cdot 20} = x^{80}\) and also to rewrite \((y^5)^{20} = y^{5 \cdot 20} = y^{100}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{(x^4y^5)^{20}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, we do similar work in the denominator: we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^2y^3)^{10} = (x^2)^{10} (y^3)^{10}\text{,}\) and then use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \((x^2)^{10} = x^{2 \cdot 10} = x^{20}\) and also to rewrite \((y^3)^{10} = y^{3 \cdot 10} = y^{30}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The next step we discuss is often glossed over, but we wanted to be thorough here. Recall that when simplifying the product of two fractions, we can just multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators. We can do this in reverse, too: when we see a single fraction, we can turn this into a product of two fractions: \(\frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = \frac{x^{80}}{x^{20}} \cdot \frac{y^{100}}{y^{30}}\)
    🔗
    Next, we use the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) to rewrite \(\frac{x^{80}}{x^{20}} = x^{80-20} = x^{60}\text{,}\) and also to rewrite \(\frac{y^{100}}{y^{30}} = y^{100-30} = y^{70}\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = x^{60} y^{70}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In summary,
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10} = \frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^4y^5)^{20}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^4)^{20} (y^5)^{20}}{(x^2)^{10} (y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = x^{60} y^{70}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    We could have started by using \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\) to rewrite \((\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3})^{10} = \frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then, we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^4y^5)^2\) as \((x^4)^2 (y^5)^2 = x^{4 \cdot 2} y^{5 \cdot 2} = x^8 y^{10}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^8 y^{10})^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^8 y^{10})^{10} = (x^8)^{10} (y^{10})^{10}\text{,}\) and then use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \((x^8)^{10} = x^{8 \cdot 10} = x^{80}\) and also to rewrite \((y^{10})^{10} = y^{10 \cdot 10} = y^{100}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{(x^8 y^{10})^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Similarly, in the denominator, we use the exponent rule \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) to rewrite \((x^2y^3)^{10} = (x^2)^{10} (y^3)^{10}\text{,}\) and then use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\) to rewrite \((x^2)^{10} = x^{2 \cdot 10} = x^{20}\) and also to rewrite \((y^3)^{10} = y^{3 \cdot 10} = y^{30}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Applying the multiplication of fractions “backwards” just as we did in the previous answer, \(\frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = \frac{x^{80}}{x^{20}} \cdot \frac{y^{100}}{y^{30}}\)
    🔗
    Next, we use the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) to rewrite \(\frac{x^{80}}{x^{20}} = x^{80-20} = x^{60}\text{,}\) and also to rewrite \(\frac{y^{100}}{y^{30}} = y^{100-30} = y^{70}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = x^{60} y^{70}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In summary,
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{(x^4y^5)^2}{x^2y^3}\right)^{10} = \frac{((x^4y^5)^2)^{10}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^4y^5)^{20}}{(x^2y^3)^{10}} = \frac{(x^4)^{20} (y^5)^{20}}{(x^2)^{10} (y^3)^{10}} = \frac{x^{80} y^{100}}{x^{20} y^{30}} = x^{60} y^{70}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that this third solution is very similar to the second solution, except that in the second solution, we first simplified \((x^4y^5)^2\) before simplifying \((x^2y^3)^{10}\text{,}\) while in this third solution, we first simplified \((x^2y^3)^{10}\) before simplifying \((x^4y^5)^2\text{.}\) Both approaches work equally well.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    An example like this one shows that there are often multiple ways to approach a problem, and that is okay. We just showed three different ways to answer the problem, all leading to the same final answer. These didn’t even all start exactly the same way! The first solution started by zooming quite far in, simplifying \((x^4y^5)^2\text{,}\) while the second and third solutions started by rewriting the entire expression using \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\text{.}\) All three answers (and in fact, additional answers that we didn’t provide) are all great, and each takes about the same amount of time.

    🔗
    Solution.
    • Using the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) in Principle 1.5.22, we have \(\frac{u^3}{u^6} = u^{3-6} = u^{-3}\text{.}\) The only exponent fact/concept we used was \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Using Principle 1.5.1, we can expand \(\frac{u^3}{u^6} = \frac{u \cdot u \cdot u}{u \cdot u \cdot u \cdot u \cdot u \cdot u}\text{.}\) Then, we can cancel three factors of \(u\) from the numerator and denominator, getting \(\frac{1}{u \cdot u \cdot u} = \frac{1}{u^3}\text{.}\) While we applied cancellation rules for fractions, the only exponent fact/concept we used was what an exponent means from Principle 1.5.1.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Following the instructions, we saw that \(\frac{u^3}{u^6}\) became either \(u^{-3}\) or became \(\frac{1}{u^3}\text{.}\) All of our work was valid. Therefore, \(u^{-3} = \frac{1}{u^3}\text{.}\) In fact, our conclusion of \(u^{-3} = \frac{1}{u^3}\) is an example of the exponent rule \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}\) in Principle 1.5.30 at play, and this activity gives us reason to believe \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}\text{.}\)

    Just a note that \(\frac{u^3}{u^6}\) does not become \(u^{1/2}\text{.}\) For more info, see Warning 1.5.26.

    Example 1.5.36.

    Simplify \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^{-2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    One option is to use \(a^{-n} = \frac1{a^n}\) to turn \(u^{-2}\) into \(\frac1{u^2}\text{.}\) Then the original expression \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^{-2}}\) is equal to \(\frac{u^{14}}{\frac{1}{u^2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we wish to avoid the look of the fraction inside the fraction (the overall expression is a fraction whose denominator is itself a fraction), we can instead write the expression \(u^{14} \div \frac{1}{u^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Instead of dividing a non-fraction and a fraction, we can rewrite this \(\frac{u^{14}}{1} \div \frac{1}{u^2}\text{,}\) which is a division of two fractions, which then becomes \(\frac{u^{14}}{1} \cdot \frac{u^2}{1}\text{,}\) where we multiply across to get \(\frac{u^{14} \cdot u^2}{1 \cdot 1} = \frac{u^{16}}{1} = u^{16}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we present all the work using equal signs (and skipping a reasonable number of steps from above), we have \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^{-2}} = \frac{u^{14}}{\frac{1}{u^2}} = \frac{u^{14}}{1} \div \frac{1}{u^2} = \frac{u^{14}}{1} \cdot \frac{u^2}{1} = u^{16}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Instead of zooming in to replace \(u^{-2}\) with \(\frac1{u^2}\text{,}\) we can instead completely zoom out, looking at the whole expression, and use the exponent rule \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) to replace the entire expression \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^{-2}}\) with \(u^{14 - (-2)} = u^{14 + 2} = u^{16}\text{.}\) Note that we wrote the exponent first as \(14 - (-2)\) before simplifying to \(14 + 2\text{.}\) Why are there two minus signs? One minus sign is the minus sign that’s already built into the formula \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{,}\) and the other minus sign is the one that is part of the exponent \(-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 1.5.37.

    Just a note that \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^{-2}}\) does not become \(u^{14-2}\) using \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{.}\) Instead, a different expression, namely \(\frac{u^{14}}{u^2}\text{,}\) becomes \(u^{14-2}\) using \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{.}\)q

    Note 1.5.38.

    In Principle 1.5.2 we saw the formula \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\text{.}\) In Principle 1.5.14 we saw the formula \((ab)^n=a^n b^n\text{.}\) The first formula tells us that when we have an expression of the form \(a^m a^n\) we can replace it with the equal expression \(a^{m+n}\text{.}\) However, we can also go in reverse: if we see an expression of the form \(a^{m+n}\text{,}\) we can replace it with the equal expression \(a^m a^n\text{.}\) The second formula tells us that when we have an expression of the form \((ab)^n\) we can replace it with the equal expression \(a^n b^n\text{.}\) However, we can also go in reverse: if we see an expression of the form \(a^n b^n\text{,}\) we can replace it with the equal expression \((ab)^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we compare the left side of the first formula and the right side of the second formula, they have the same “shape” to them. What I mean is that both expressions \(a^m a^n\) and \(a^n b^n\) happen to be a base raised to an exponent, multiplied by a base raised to an exponent. So, we really have to pay attention to which variable(s) are repeated and which variable(s) are not repeated. For example, in \(a^m a^n\text{,}\) the base \(a\) is repeated, while in \(a^n b^n\text{,}\) the exponent \(n\) is repeated.
    🔗
    Thus, in the expression \(3^c 10^c\text{,}\) both exponents are the same, meaning that the formula which has \(a^n b^n\) on one side applies, since \(a^n b^n\) also has exponents which match. In the expression \(\sqrt{7}^k \sqrt{7}^4\text{,}\) the bases are the same, so the formula which has \(a^m a^n\) on one side applies, since \(a^m a^n\) also has bases which match.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.39.

    Rewrite \(3^c 10^c\) using a relevant exponent formula.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since both exponents are the same, we can use the formula \((ab)^n = a^n b^n\) in Principle 1.5.14 to rewrite \(3^c 10^c = (3 \cdot 10)^c = 30^c\text{.}\) While we have practiced using the formula \((ab)^n = a^n b^n\) to turn an expression of the form \((ab)^n\) into an expression of the form \(a^n b^n\) (see for example Example 1.5.17), we can also use the formula in reverse, turning an expression of the form \(a^n b^n\) into an expression of the form \((ab)^n\text{,}\) like we just did in this example.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.40.

    Rewrite \(\sqrt{7}^k \sqrt{7}^4\) using a relevant exponent formula.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since both bases are the same (they happen to be \(\sqrt{7}\)), we can use the formula \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\) in Principle 1.5.2 to rewrite \(\sqrt{7}^k \sqrt{7}^4 = \sqrt{7}^{k+4}\text{.}\) In this example, the new exponent \(k+4\) does not simplify further. In another example like \(u^3 u^5\text{,}\) we can apply the formula to get \(u^{3+5}\text{,}\) or even skip writing this step, and in either case we could simplify the new exponent \(3+5\) to \(8\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Note 1.5.41.

    In Principle 1.5.14 we saw the formula \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\text{.}\) In Principle 1.5.22 we saw the formula \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\text{.}\) The right side of the first formula and the left side of the second formula have the same “shape” to them. However, in \(\frac{a^n}{b^n}\) the exponents match, while in \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) the bases match.
    🔗
    Thus, in the expression \(\frac{20^c}{5^c}\text{,}\) both exponents are the same, meaning that the formula which has \(\frac{a^n}{b^n}\) on one side applies, since \(\frac{a^n}{b^n}\) also has exponents which match. In the expression \(\frac{p^8}{p^2}\text{,}\) the bases are the same, so the formula which has \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) on one side applies, since \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) also has bases which match.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.42.

    Rewrite \(\frac{20^c}{5^c}\) using a relevant exponent formula.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since both exponents are the same, we can use the formula \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\) in Principle 1.5.14 to rewrite \(\frac{20^c}{5^c} = (\frac{20}{5})^c = 4^c\text{.}\) While we have practiced using the formula \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\) to turn an expression of the form \((\frac{a}{b})^n\) into an expression of the form \(\frac{a^n}{b^n}\) (see for example AAAAA), we can also use the formula in reverse, turning an expression of the form \(\frac{a^n}{b^n}\) into an expression of the form \((\frac{a}{b})^n\text{,}\) like we just did in this example.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.43.

    Rewrite \(\frac{p^8}{p^2}\) using a relevant exponent formula.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since both bases are the same (they happen to be \(p\)), we can use the formula \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) in Principle 1.5.22 to rewrite \(\frac{p^8}{p^2} = p^{8-2} = p^6\text{.}\) While we have practiced using the formula \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) to turn an expression of the form \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\) into an expression of the form \(a^{m-n}\) (see for example AAAA), we can also use the formula in reverse, turning an expression of the form \(a^{m-n}\) into an expression of the form \(\frac{a^m}{a^n}\text{,}\) like we just did in this example.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that a common mistake is to turn \(\frac{p^8}{p^2}\) into \(p^4\text{.}\) However, there is no exponent rule that justifies this. In fact, among all the exponent rules we have seen up to this point, there is never a time that one exponent gets divided by another exponent.

    Warning 1.5.44.

    It is a very common error to turn an expression of the form \((x+y)^n\) into an expression of the from \(x^n + y^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Here are specific examples of what this warning is trying to address:
    • We cannot turn \((x+y)^2\) into \(x^2 + y^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • We cannot turn \((u+7)^9\) into \(u^9 + 7^9\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • We cannot turn \((2+k)^c\) into \(2^c + k^c\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    We have seen various versions of the distributive property in AAAAAA. For example, specific versions included \(a(b+c) = ab + ac\) and \((b+c)a = ba + ca\text{.}\) It is true that in our examples where we said that we couldn’t “distribute”, we had addition inside the parentheses. So, what’s the problem? Why doesn’t the distributive rule apply? The issue is that in the format of \(a(b+c)\text{,}\) we have to be very careful to pay attention the operation between \(a\) and the content in parentheses. In \(a(b+c)\text{,}\) the operation between \(a\) and \((b+c)\) is multiplication. In other words, \(a\) is a factor that is multiplied. The Distributive Law only addresses the situation where a factor is multiplied by a sum or a difference. The point is that it is not enough to just think that anything outside of parentheses can be “distributed” to everything inside the parentheses. In all three of our examples above, the thing outside of parentheses is an exponent, not a factor.
    🔗
    The Distributive Law only applies to the situation that it is describing through its formula, and nothing else. This is why we have to pay close attention to the formula as written: we examine each formula for what operations are occuring, where these operations are occuring, and any repeated variables. If we don’t pay close attention to these details, we may misapply a formula.
    🔗
    We have introduced the exponent rule \((ab)^n = a^n b^n\) in Principle 1.5.14. In this formula, the operation between \(a\) and \(b\) is multiplication, and the role of the thing outside of parentheses \(is\) an exponent. So, this formula tells us that if we have multiplication inside the parentheses and an exponent outside, the exponent can apply to each of the factors.
    🔗
    We have also introduced the exponent rule \((\frac{a}{b})^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}\) in Principle 1.5.14. In this formula, the operation between \(a\) and \(b\) is division, and the role of the thing outside of parentheses \(is\) an exponent. So, this formula tells us that if we have division inside the parentheses and an exponent outside, the exponent can apply to both the numerator and denominator.
    🔗

    Subsection Radicals

    Definition 1.5.45. What does a radical mean?

    \(\sqrt[n]{a}\) asks for the value that fills in the blank:
    \begin{equation*} (\_\_\_)^n = a\text{.} \end{equation*}
    If two numbers can fill in the blank (positive and negative), then by definition, the positive number is the answer. The answer to the radical question identifies the value of the base.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.46.

    Find \(\sqrt[2]{64}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This asks for the value that fills in the blank:
    \begin{equation*} (\_\_\_)^2 = 64\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Both \(8\) and \(-8\) fill in the blank, but by definition, the positive number is the answer. Therefore, \(\sqrt[2]{64} = 8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.47.

    Find \(\sqrt[3]{64}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This asks for the value that fills in the blank:
    \begin{equation*} (\_\_\_)^3 = 64\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The only number that fills in the blank is \(4\text{.}\) (The reason that \(-4\) does not fill in the blank is that \((-4)^3 = -64\text{,}\) not \(64\text{.}\)) Therefore, \(\sqrt[3]{64} = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.48.

    Find \(\sqrt[2]{25}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    When we raise the base \(5\) to the power \(2\text{,}\) the result is \(25\text{.}\) So the base \(5\) answers the question. Written as notation with an equal sign properly, \(\sqrt[2]{25} = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 1.5.49.

    It would be incorrect to write
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[2]{25} = \sqrt[2]{5}. \end{equation*}
    Of course, the completion to the right of the equal sign should have a \(5\text{,}\) so when someone writes \(\sqrt[2]{5}\text{,}\) it is good to see the \(5\) present, since they are thinking about the correct base. However, the radical should not surround the \(5\) because we have already processed what the radical does.
    🔗
    🔗

    Principle 1.5.50. Radical missing small number.

    \(\sqrt{a} = \sqrt[2]{a}\)
    🔗
    If the small number outside of the radical is missing, it is a hidden \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We do this because the small number outside of the radical is often (but not always) a \(2\text{.}\) In other words, we often need to find the base when the exponent is \(2\text{,}\) but there are also times we need to find the base when the exponent is something else, like \(3\) or \(4\) or \(5\text{,}\) etc.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.51.

    Find \(\sqrt{25}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This question is asking \(\sqrt[2]{25}\text{,}\) so \(\sqrt{25} = \sqrt[2]{25} = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 1.5.52.

    Like in Warning 1.5.49, it would be incorrect to write
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{25} = \sqrt{5}. \end{equation*}
    Instead, we should write
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{25} = 5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.53.

    Simplify \(\sqrt{81}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    \(\sqrt{81} = \sqrt[2]{81} = 9\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    \(\sqrt{81} = 9\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Note, in the second answer, we skipped writing the middle expression \(\sqrt[2]{81}\text{.}\) This is fine to do, but it’s good to remember that when the little number is missing outside of the radical, it is really a hidden \(2\text{.}\) The reason we’re taking time to say this is that sometimes people breeze through then the hidden number is a \(2\text{,}\) but then struggle when there’s another number there such as a \(3\text{.}\) The point is that Definition 1.5.45 gives us a uniform and consistent way to think about all radicals, whether the small number outside of the radical is a \(2\) (missing or written) or another number is written there such as a \(3\) or \(4\text{.}\) In all cases, a radical is asking us to identify the base while the little number outside the radical is telling us the exponent (and the content inside the radical is telling us the result of taking the base raised to the exponent).
    🔗

    Language 1.5.54. How to say radicals.

    \(\sqrt[n]{a}\) should be said “\(n\)-th root of \(a\)”. When the value of \(n\) is small, there are also special names for these:
    • \(\sqrt[2]{a}\) or \(\sqrt{a}\) can be said “\(2\)nd root of \(a\)” or “square root of \(a\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\sqrt[3]{a}\) is spoken “\(3\)rd root of \(a\)” or “cube root of \(a\)”, but not “\(3\) square root of \(a\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
    For other values of \(n\text{,}\) we just say “\(n\)-th root of \(a\)”. For example:
    • \(\sqrt[4]{a}\) is spoken “\(4\)th root of \(a\)” but not “\(4\) square root of \(a\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\sqrt[7]{a}\) is spoken “\(7\)th root of \(a\)” but not “\(7\) square root of \(a\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 1.5.55.

    It is tempting to look at \(\sqrt[7]{5}\) and want to say “seven square root of five”, but saying “seven square root of five” actually refers to \(7 \sqrt{5}\) which can also be written \(7 \sqrt[2]{5}\text{.}\) The point is that \(\sqrt[7]{5}\) and \(7 \sqrt{5}\) are different values:
    • \(\sqrt[7]{5}\) asks for what is the base that when raised to the exponent \(7\) results in the value of \(5\text{.}\) Using a calculator, I found this to be approximately \(1.258\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(7\sqrt{5}\) asks for what is the base that when raised to the exponent \(2\) results in the value of \(5\text{,}\) and then multiplies that result by \(7\text{.}\) Using a calculator, I found \(\sqrt{5}\) is approximately \(2.236\text{.}\) I then had to multiply this by \(7\text{.}\) Thus, the value of \(7\sqrt{5}\) is approximately \(15.652\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Principle 1.5.56.

    \(\sqrt[n]{a^n} = a\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In words, this formula says that an \(n\)th root and an \(n\)th power undo each other.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.57.

    Find \(\sqrt{81}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Inside the radical is \(81\text{,}\) which we can replace with \(9^2\text{,}\) since \(9^2\) and \(81\) are equal. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{81} = \sqrt[2]{81} = \sqrt[2]{9^2}. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the above, we still had to write the second root: we have not processed the root here yet (we have only replaced \(81\) with something that is equal to \(81\)). Now, we can use the formula \(\sqrt[n]{a^n} = a\) to process the radical: \(\sqrt[2]{9^2} = 9\text{.}\) Presenting all the work with equal signs, we have
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{81} = \sqrt[2]{81} = \sqrt[2]{9^2} = 9. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    It may seem strange to include the step of writing \(\sqrt[2]{9^2}\) when we already provided two perfectly good solutions in Example 1.5.53, but there are two reasons we are doing this:
    1. Including this step makes it easier to illustrate the use of the formula \(\sqrt[n]{a^n} = a\) in Principle 1.5.56.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This gives us yet another way to simplify an expression containing a radical, and while this isn’t most people’s preferred way of thinking about \(\sqrt{81}\text{,}\) this new method is preferred by some people for certain problems that we will soon see, including the cube root problem below.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.58.

    Find \(\sqrt[3]{64}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Inside the radical is \(64\text{,}\) which we can replace with \(4^3\text{,}\) since \(4^3\) and \(64\) are equal. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{64} = \sqrt[3]{4^3}. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the above, we still had to write the third root: we have not processed the root here yet (we have only replaced \(64\) with something that is equal to \(64\)). Now, we can use the formula \(\sqrt[n]{a^n} = a\) to process the radical: \(\sqrt[3]{4^3} = 4\text{.}\) Presenting all the work with equal signs, we have
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{64} = \sqrt[3]{4^3} = 4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In the setting of algebra, we will need to simplify the root of expressions that contain variables.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.59.

    Simplify \(\sqrt{x^{10}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the little number outside the radical is missing, \(\sqrt{x^{10}} = \sqrt[2]{x^{10}}\text{.}\) So, this is asking for the base that when raised to the exponent \(2\) results in the value of \(x^{10}\text{.}\) That is, we are being asked to fill in the blank in \((\_\_\_)^2 = x^{10}\text{.}\) Here are three valid ways that we can think about this:
    • We can use the exponent rule \((a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n}\) in Principle 1.5.2 in reverse. The expression \(x^{10}\) can be rewritten as \((x^5)^2\text{,}\) since \(5 \cdot 2 = 10\text{.}\) The missing base is \(x^5\text{,}\) since \((x^5)^2 = x^{10}\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\sqrt{x^{10}} = \sqrt[2]{(x^5)^2} = x^5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • We can think about what the exponent \(2\) means (or in other words, we can think about what squaring means). Squaring a thing means to multiply the thing by itself. So, we ask ourselves: what thing can be multiplied by itself to get \(x^{10}\text{?}\) More visually, how do we fill in the blanks in \((\_\_\_)(\_\_\_) = x^{10}\) with the restriction that both blanks need to be filled in with the same thing? Informed by the exponent rule \(a^m a^n = a^{m+n}\) in Principle 1.5.2 which says that when we multiply two things with the same base we add the exponents, both blanks can be filled in with \(x^5\text{.}\) That is, \(x^5 \cdot x^5 = x^{10}\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\sqrt{x^{10}} = \sqrt[2]{(x^5)^2} = x^5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • We can also apply the formula \(\sqrt[n]{a^n} = a\) in Principle 1.5.56, and emulate the work we did in Example 1.5.57. The content inside the radical, which is \(x^{10}\text{,}\) can be replaced with \((x^5)^2\text{,}\) since \((x^5)^2\) and \(x^{10}\) are equal. Thus,
      \begin{equation*} \sqrt{x^{10}} = \sqrt[2]{x^{10}} = \sqrt[2]{(x^5)^2} = x^5. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.60.

    With proper notation, answer both questions below as similarly as possible, using Principle 1.5.56:
    1. Simplify \(\sqrt[2]{c^{18}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Simplify \(\sqrt[2]{49}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle \sqrt[2]{c^{18}} = \sqrt[2]{(c^9)^2} = c^9\)
      🔗

    Principle 1.5.61.

    \(\sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a} \sqrt[n]{b}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In words, this formula says that an \(n\)th root of a product can be rewritten as the product of the \(n\)th roots of each factor. In fact, this may be slightly clearer by writing the same formula, but including the hidden multiplication signs:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[n]{a \cdot b} = \sqrt[n]{a} \cdot \sqrt[n]{b}. \end{equation*}
    Either version of the formula (with hidden multiplication signs or written multiplication signs) is very useful when we need to simplify a radical that contains a product inside the radical. The key is that all roots have to be the same kind: this formula doesn’t allow mixing a square root and a cube root, for example. To be clear, special cases of this formula include the version for square roots:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \sqrt{b} \end{equation*}
    and the version for cube roots:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a} \sqrt[3]{b} \end{equation*}
    but there’s also a version for fourth roots and fifth roots and so on. (Each of these special versions can also be written where we write the hidden multiplication signs. I encourage you to write the two previous equations with multiplication signs included, since multiplication needs to be present to apply this formula.)

    Example 1.5.62.

    Simplify \(\sqrt{18}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We examine the content inside the radical (which in this example is \(18\)) and ask how we can write \(18\) as the product, where one of the factors of the product is a perfect square. We do need a product (since the left side of the formula in Principle 1.5.61 says \(\sqrt[n]{ab}\) and not \(\sqrt[n]{a+b}\)). So, while \(18=16+2\) is true and one of the pieces (namely \(16\)) is a perfect square, this isn’t really going to help us. We want to write \(18\) as a product with one piece being a perfect square, and a useful way to do that is \(18= 9 \cdot 2\text{.}\) So,
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2}. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the work shown above, we still wrote the square root sign, because all we did was replace \(18\) with the equal value of \(9 \cdot 2\text{.}\) We haven’t processed the square root yet. Now, \(\sqrt{9 \cdot 2}\) is in the format of the left side of the formula \(\sqrt{a \cdot b} = \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b}\) So, we can apply this:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{9} \cdot \sqrt{2}. \end{equation*}
    Now, in the writing of \(\sqrt{9} \cdot \sqrt{2}\text{,}\) we can simplify \(\sqrt{9}\) because \(9\) is a perfect square, but we have to leave \(\sqrt{2}\) alone. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{9} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 3 \cdot \sqrt{2}. \end{equation*}
    We now present a summary of all the work, writing equal signs where they belong, though we will leave out multiplication symbols where possible to save a little time: \(\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{9} \sqrt{2} = 3 \sqrt{2}\text{.}\)
