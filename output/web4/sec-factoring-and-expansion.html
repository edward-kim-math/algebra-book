    Subsection Expansion

    In this section, we will look at products of binomials, which are expressions of the format \((a+b)(c+d)\text{.}\) As before, a product means the result of multiplication, and that multiplication happens between the value of \((a+b)\) and the vaule of \((c+d)\text{.}\) By binomial, we mean \((a+b)\) is a binomial, and \((c+d)\) is a binomial. We start with expansion, where we turn this product of binomials \((a+b)(c+d)\) into an expression of the format \(Ax^2+Bx+C\text{,}\) known as a trinomial. Because the highest power is a \(2\text{,}\) we note that \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) is a quadratic. In \(Ax^2+Bx+C\text{,}\) we will call \(A\) the quadratic coefficient, call \(B\) the linear coefficient, and call \(C\) the constant term. For example, in \(5x^2+6x+7\text{,}\) the quadratic coefficient is \(5\text{,}\) the linear coefficient is \(6\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We will start with \((a+b)(c+d)\) where both \(a\) and \(c\) are the variable \(x\text{.}\) This will lead to the quadratic coefficient \(A\) in \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) always being \(1\text{.}\) In these all such examples, we will start with expressions of the form \((a+b)(c+d)\text{,}\) but then look at the expansion \(Ax^2+Bx+C\text{,}\) comparing the expansions from several examples to see if there are any patterns. Spoiler alert: some of the patterns can be come about from examining which coefficients in \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) are positive and which coefficients are negative.
    🔗
    Let’s take our initial Distributive Law \(a(b+c) = ab + ac\text{,}\) for the sake of clarity, temporarily replace the letters \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) with \(x\) and \(y\) and \(z\) instead. It is technically the same formula to have
    \begin{equation*} x(y+z) = xy + xz \end{equation*}
    but in this version of the Distributive Law, we will subsitute \(x=a+b\) and \(y=c\) and \(z=d\) to get
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)(c+d) = (a+b)c + (a+b)d. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the equation above, the left side is \((a+b)(c+d)\) instead of \(a+b(c+d)\) due to the Order of Operations: we really needed the value of \(x\text{,}\) which is \(a+b\text{,}\) to be multiplied by \((c+d)\text{.}\) Due to the fact that the Order of Operations says to do multiplication before addition, we need to use parentheses to protect the value of \(a+b\text{,}\) since it is the value of \(a+b\) that needs to be multiplied by \(c+d\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the right side of the equation above, we have \((a+b)c + (a+b)d\text{,}\) and we can apply the Distributive Law to rewrite \((a+b)c\) as \(ac + bc\text{,}\) and rewrite \((a+b)d\) as \(ad + bd\text{.}\) Putting all the pieces together, we have
    \begin{equation*} (a+b)(c+d) = ac + bc + ad + bd. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Principle 1.6.1. Expanding the Product of Two Binomials.

    \begin{equation*} (a+b)(c+d) = ac + ad + bc + bd \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.2.

    Expand and simplify \((x+3)(x+5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use Principle 1.6.1 with \(a=x\) and \(b=3\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=5\) to get
    \begin{equation*} (x+3)(x+5) = x\cdot x + x\cdot 5 + 3\cdot x + 3\cdot 5 = x^2 + 5x + 3x + 15 = x^2 + 8x + 15. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.3.

    Because it will be very useful later, we examine the final expression \(x^2+8x+15\) identifying that it is in the form \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) by noting the quadratic coefficient is \(A=1\text{,}\) the linear coefficient \(B=8\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(C=15\text{.}\) It is very important to note that the linear coefficient \(8\) is the sum of the numbers \(3\) and \(5\) in the original expression, while the constant term \(15\) is the product of the numbers \(3\) and \(5\) in the original expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.4.

    Expand and simplify \((x+4)(x+10)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use Principle 1.6.1 with \(a=x\) and \(b=4\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=10\) to get
    \begin{equation*} (x+4)(x+10) = x\cdot x + x\cdot 10 + 4\cdot x + 4\cdot 10 = x^2 + 10x + 4x + 40 = x^2 + 14x + 40. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.5.

    Because it will be very useful later, we examine the final expression \(x^2+14x+40\) identifying that it is in the form \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) by noting the quadratic coefficient is \(A=1\text{,}\) the linear coefficient \(B=14\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(C=40\text{.}\) It is very important to note that the linear coefficient \(14\) is the sum of the numbers \(4\) and \(10\) in the original expression, while the constant term \(40\) is the product of the numbers \(4\) and \(10\) in the original expression.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.6.

    Expand and simplify \((x+5)(x+5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x+5)(x+5) = x^2 + 5x + 5x + 25 = x^2 + 10x + 25.\)

    🔗

    Warning 1.6.7.

    Principle 1.6.10.

    \begin{equation*} (a-b)(c-d) = ac - ad - bc + bd \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Whether you choose to use this version \((a-b)(c-d) = ac - ad - bc + bd\) directly or think via Principle 1.6.1 is a matter of personal choice. Let’s demonstrate the two ways to think about this through an example.
    🔗

    Example 1.6.11.

    Expand and simplify \((x-3)(x-8)\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The given expression \((x-3)(x-8)\) fits the format of the left side \((a-b)(c-d)\) in Principle 1.6.10 with \(a=x\) and \(b=3\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=8\text{.}\) In doing these identifications, the confusion may be why the stating of \(b\) and \(d\) do not have minus signs, but this is because the format of \((a-b)(c-d)\) already includes minus signs. Then, by Principle 1.6.10,
    \begin{equation*} (x-3)(x-8) = x \cdot x - x \cdot 8 - 3 \cdot x + 3 \cdot 8 = x^2 - 8x - 3x + 24 = x^2 - 11x + 24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    We can rewrite the expression \((x-3)(x-8) = (x+(-3))(x+(-8))\) because \((x+(-3))(x+(-8))\) fits the format of the left side \((a+b)(c+d)\) in Principle 1.6.1. In this format, \(a=x\text{,}\) and \(b\) should consist of content right after the first plus sign, so \(b=-3\text{.}\) Similarly, \(c=x\text{,}\) and \(d\) should be all the content after the second plus sign, so \(d=-8\text{.}\) Then by Principle 1.6.1,
    \begin{equation*} (x+(-3))(x+(-8)) = x \cdot x + x \cdot (-8) + (-3) \cdot x + (-3)(-8) = x^2 - 8x - 3x + 24 = x^2-11x+24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.12.

    Because it will be very useful later, we examine the final expression \(x^2-11x+24\) identifying that it is in the form \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) will need us to first slightly rewrite the final expression as \(x^2 + (-11)x + 24\text{.}\) by noting the quadratic coefficient is \(A=1\text{,}\) the linear coefficient \(B=-11\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(C=24\text{.}\) It is very important to note that the linear coefficient \(-11\) is the sum of the numbers \(-3\) and \(-8\) in the original expression, while the constant term \(24\) is the product of the numbers \(-3\) and \(-8\) in the original expression. (While we noted \((-3)+(-8)=-11\) we can also note \(3+8=11\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    Before moving on to another example, we note it is a matter of personal taste whether there really was a need to introduce a separate formula as Principle 1.6.10. You may prefer to apply principles such as a negative times a negative results in a positive, without needing to introduce a variant of Principle 1.6.1.
    🔗

    Example 1.6.13.

    Expand and simplify \((x-4)(x-6)\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The given expression \((x-4)(x-6)\) fits the format of the left side \((a-b)(c-d)\) in Principle 1.6.10 with \(a=x\) and \(b=4\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=6\text{.}\) In doing these identifications, the confusion may be why the stating of \(b\) and \(d\) do not have minus signs, but this is because the format of \((a-b)(c-d)\) already includes minus signs. Then, by Principle 1.6.10,
    \begin{equation*} (x-4)(x-6) = x \cdot x - x \cdot 6 - 4 \cdot x + 4 \cdot 6 = x^2 - 6x - 4x + 24 = x^2 - 10x + 24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    We can rewrite the expression \((x-4)(x-6) = (x+(-4))(x+(-6))\) because \((x+(-4))(x+(-6))\) fits the format of the left side \((a+b)(c+d)\) in Principle 1.6.1. In this format, \(a=x\text{,}\) and \(b\) should consist of content right after the first plus sign, so \(b=-4\text{.}\) Similarly, \(c=x\text{,}\) and \(d\) should be all the content after the second plus sign, so \(d=-6\text{.}\) Then by Principle 1.6.1,
    \begin{equation*} (x+(-4))(x+(-6)) = x \cdot x + x \cdot (-6) + (-4) \cdot x + (-4)(-6) = x^2 - 6x - 4x + 24 = x^2-10x+24. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.14.

    In \(x^2-10x+24\text{,}\) we can rewrite the final expression as \(x^2 + (-10)x + 24\text{,}\) which has linear coefficient \(-10\) and constant term \(24\text{.}\) Note that the constant \(24\) is the product of the numbers \((-4)\) and \((-6)\) in the original expression (or we can point out that the product of the numbers after the minus signs, namely \(4\) and \(6\) multiply to \(24\)). Thus the linear coefficient \(-10\) is the sum of the numbers \(-4\) and \(-6\text{,}\) (or we can point out that the numbers after the minus signs \(4\) and \(6\) add to \(10\text{,}\) the number after the minus sign in \(x^2-10x+24\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Expand and simplify \((x-5)(x-5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x-5)(x-5) = x^2 - 5x - 5x + 25 = x^2 - 10x + 25.\)

    Example 1.6.16.

    Expand and simplify \((x-8)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x-8)^2=(x-8)(x-8) = x^2 - 8x - 8x + 64 = x^2 - 16x + 64.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We present two formulas together because they are similar: in each, the left side is a product of two binomials, with one binomial having a plus sign in between, and one binomial having a minus sign in between.

    Principle 1.6.17.

    • \(\displaystyle (a+b)(c-d) = ac - ad + bc - bd\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (a-b)(c+d) = ac + ad - bc - bd\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.18.

    Expand and simplify \((x+7)(x-2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use the first formula in Principle 1.6.17 with \(a=x\) and \(b=7\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=2\) to get
    \begin{equation*} (x+7)(x-2) = x\cdot x - x\cdot 2 + 7\cdot x - 7\cdot 2 = x^2 - 2x + 7x - 14 = x^2 + 5x - 14. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.19.

    For the first time in this section, we end with a quadratic expression \(x^2 + 5x - 14\) where the constant term is negative. This happened because we multiplied \(+7\) with \(-2\text{.}\) Recall the product of a real numbers with opposite signs results in a negative number. In the original expression, let’s examine the \(7\) and the \(2\) which appear after the plus sign and the minus sign. The difference between these numbers is \(5\text{,}\) and note the linear coefficient was \(5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.20.

    Expand and simplify \((x-7)(x+2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use the second formula in Principle 1.6.17 with \(a=x\) and \(b=7\) and \(c=x\) and \(d=2\) to get
    \begin{equation*} (x-7)(x+2) = x\cdot x + x\cdot 2 - 7\cdot x - 7\cdot 2 = x^2 + 2x - 7x - 14 = x^2 - 5x - 14. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.6.21.

    As in our previous example, the final expression \(x^2 - 5x - 14\) has a negative constant term, for the same reason as earlier: this was the result of multiplying a negative number (namely \(-7\)) together with a positive number (namely \(+2\)). The opposite of the linear coefficient \(-5\) is \(5\text{,}\) and that is the difference between the numbers \(7\) and \(2\) in the original expression. When we subtract \(2\) and \(7\) in a certain order, we get \(-5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.22.

    Expand and simplify \((x-3)(x+10)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x-3)(x+10) = x^2 + 10x - 3x - 30 = x^2 + 7x - 30\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6.23.

    Expand and simplify \((x+9)(x-9)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \((x+9)(x-9) = x^2 - 9x + 9x - 81 = x^2 + 0x - 81 = x^2 - 81\text{.}\)

    Note 1.6.24.

    We have been looking at expanding and simplifying the product of binomials. Typically in these questions, the expansion creates four terms and then collecting of like terms leads to a total of three terms. In this case, we only have two terms in \(x^2-81\) because the middle terms \(-9x+9x\) simplified to zero.

    Subsection Factoring

    Our goal here is to take a trinomial in the format of \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) such as \(x^2+7x-30\) and \(x^2+14x+40\) and factor this, producing a product of binomials. We start with the situation where \(A=1\text{.}\) It is helpful to see what happens based on whether the constant term is positive or negative.

    Strategy 1.6.28. Factoring a quadratic trinomial into a product of binomials.

    Suppose we are given \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) with \(A=1\text{,}\) meaning we have \(x^2+Bx+C\text{.}\) To factor \(x^2+Bx+C\) as the product of binomials:
    • If the costant term \(C\) is positive, then the signs contained in both binomials match.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If the costant term \(C\) is negative, then the signs contained in both binomials differ.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now, let’s dig in further to see what happens when the constant term is postive. First, let’s note that when we have the consstant term positive, then the two binomials have to have the same sign, meaning that both signs are positive or both signs are negative. But now, we can see two situations: the linear term can be positive or negative. For example, we can have \(x^2+9x+20\) or we can have \(x^2-9x+20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It seems like a reasonable guess (and we’ll check if it works) that if we have a \(+9x\) term, that the matching sign in both binomials is a plus, while if we have a \(-9x\) term, then the matching sign in both binomials is a minus.
    🔗
    Let’s dig in to see if we can find the missing values in
    \begin{equation*} (x+\_\_\_)(x+\_\_\_) = x^2+9x+20. \end{equation*}
    To match the notation earlier, let’s fill in these blanks with \(b\) and \(d\text{.}\) That is, let’s find the \(b\) and \(d\) in
    \begin{equation*} (x+b)(x+d)=x^2+9x+20 \end{equation*}
    by expanding the left side:
    \begin{equation*} x^2+dx+bx+bd = x^2+9x+20. \end{equation*}
    Now, the middle two terms \(dx\) and \(bx\) are like terms, and we can “collect like terms” here, though what we’ll really do is apply Note 1.1.84 and factor out \(x\) for the middle two terms:
    \begin{equation*} x^2+(d+b)x+bd = x^2+9x+20. \end{equation*}
    The last term on the left \(bd\) has no \(x\text{,}\) so this must be what matches up with \(20\) on the right. The linear coefficient (the number in front of \(x\)) on the left is \(d+b\) and on the right is \(9\text{.}\) This combination of things tells us what we need. The two numbers we need to find (called \(b\) and \(d\) here) need to multiply to \(20\) and need to add to \(9\text{.}\) Those two numbers are \(4\) and \(5\) so we can factor \(x^2+9x+20\) as either
    \begin{equation*} (x+4)(x+5) \end{equation*}
    or
    \begin{equation*} (x+5)(x+4). \end{equation*}
    These are essentially the same (just switching the order of the factors), and we can check our factoring by expanding.
    🔗
    For \(x^2-9x+20\text{,}\) we’ll need minus signs inside both binomials. That is, our set up looks like:
    \begin{equation*} (x-\_\_\_)(x-\_\_\_) = x^2-9x+20. \end{equation*}
    Now, the minus signs are already drawn in, so we just need to fill in numbers after the minus signs. Based on the \(20\) and the number after the minus sign but before the \(x\) being \(9\text{,}\) we are again looking for two numbers whose product is \(20\) and whose sum is \(9\text{,}\) and these are again \(4\) and \(5\text{,}\) which fill in the two blanks:
    \begin{equation*} (x-4)(x-5) \end{equation*}
    or alternately,
    \begin{equation*} (x-5)(x-4). \end{equation*}
    It is always a little more confusing to describe, which is why we are using the following language: in
    \begin{equation*} (x-\_\_\_)(x-\_\_\_) = x^2-9x+20 \end{equation*}
    we have already written in the minus signs, so we just need to include write after the minus signs the two positive numbers that add to \(9\) and multiply to \(20\text{.}\)
    🔗

    🔗
    Troll 2: Troll 1 is lying.
    🔗
    Troll 3: Either we are all knaves, or at least one of us is a knight.
    🔗
    🔗
    Which troll is which?

    Definition 1.6.36. Argument.

    An argument is a sequence of statements, the last of which is called the conclusion and the rest of which are called premises.
    🔗
    An argument is said to be valid provided the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are all true. An argument is invalid if it is not valid; that is, all the premises can be true, and the conclusion could still be false.
    🔗
    An argument is sound provided it is valid and all the premises are true. A proof of a statement is a sound argument whose conclusion is the statement.
    🔗
    🔗

    By the way...

    Our definitions of argument, valid argument, and sound argument are the same ones used in philosophy, the other primary academic discipline concerned with logic and reasoning.
    🔗

    Example 1.6.37.

    Consider the following two arguments:
    🔗
    If Edith eats her vegetables, then she can have a cookie.
    Edith eats her vegetables.
    \(\therefore\) Edith gets a cookie.
    Florence must eat her vegetables to get a cookie.
    Florence eats her vegetables.
    \(\therefore\) Florence gets a cookie.
    (The symbol “\(\therefore\)” means “therefore”)
    🔗
    Are these arguments valid?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Do you agree that the first argument is valid but the second argument is not? We will soon develop a better understanding of the logic involved in this analysis, but if your intuition agrees with this assessment, then you are in good shape.
    🔗
    Notice the two arguments look almost identical. Edith and Florence both eat their vegetables. In both cases, there is a connection between the eating of vegetables and cookies. Yet we claim that it is valid to conclude that Edith gets a cookie, but not that Florence does. The difference must be in the connection between eating vegetables and getting cookies. We need to be skilled at reading and comprehending these sentences. Do the two sentences mean the same thing?
    🔗
    Unfortunately, in everyday language we are often sloppy, and you might be tempted to say they are equivalent. But notice that just because Florence must eat her vegetables, we have not claimed that doing so would be enough (she might also need to clean her room, for example). In everyday (non-mathematical) practice, you might be tempted to say this “other direction” is implied. In mathematics, we never get that luxury.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.6.38.

    The arguments in the example above illustrate another important point: Even if you don’t care about the advancement of human knowledge in the field of mathematics, becoming skilled at analyzing arguments is useful. And even if you don’t want to give your grandmother a cookie. If you are using mathematics to solve problems in some other discipline, it is still necessary to demonstrate that your solution is correct. You better have a good argument that it is!
