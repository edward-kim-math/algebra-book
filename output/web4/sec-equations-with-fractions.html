    Section 1.4 Equations with Fractions

    Objectives

    In this section, we learn how to:
    🔗
    1. Solve equations with fractions present.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A new strategy

    In Section 1.2, we learned how to simplify expressions with fractions. In this section, we look beyond simplifying expressions with fractions, and instead look at solving equations with fractions. The last two sections discussed expressions with fractions followed by solving equations, so now it’s time to see efficient ways to solve equations with fractions. Recall from Warning 1.2.56 that simplifying expressions with fractions does not always require a common denominator. (More specifically, when asked to simplify the product of two fractions, we do not need a common denominator.) So, I invite you -- as we go through this section -- to be willing to question the idea that we’d always need a common denominator when solving equations with fractions. After all, it wasn’t every fractional expression that required common denominators: only some of them did.
    🔗

    Strategy 1.4.1. Solving an equation with fractions.

    • When does this strategy apply? This strategy applies in any equation we are asked to solve when there is one or more fractions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • How to apply the strategy
      1. Inventory all of the fractions, whether they appear on the left side, the right side, or both. In particular, identify all of the denominators present.
        🔗
        🔗
      2. Off to the side, compute the least common denominator (LCD) of all of the fractions. This phrase least common denominator is shorthand to mean the least common multiple of all of the denominators. (See APPENDIX for details on least common multiples.)
        🔗
        🔗
      3. Take the least common multiple computed in the previous step and multiply both sides of the equation by this expression. (Because we have to multiply the entire left side and the entire right side, it may be necessary to use parentheses due to the Order of Operations.)
        🔗
        🔗
      4. On each side, distribute if applicable.
        🔗
        🔗
      5. In each term on each side, cancel where applicable. This always results in an equation which has no fractions.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.2.

    Solve \(\frac{x}{3} + \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We are being asked to solve an equation with fractions, so Strategy 1.4.1 applies. First, we inventory all of the fractions and identify the denominators, which are \(3\) and \(5\) and \(10\text{.}\) The least common multiple of \(3\) and \(5\) and \(10\) is \(30\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We multiply both sides of the equation by \(30\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 30 \left( \frac{x}{3} + \frac{2}{5} \right) = 30 \cdot \frac{3}{10}. \end{equation*}
    Note that on the left side of the equation, we used parentheses to ensure that the entire left side is multiplied by \(30\text{:}\) the Order of Operations tells us that multiplication is performed before addition, so without parentheses, only the first fraction would be multiplied by \(30\text{.}\) On the right side, we could have drawn parentheses around the entire right side, but because there is only one term on the right side, it was not necessary.
    🔗
    Now, distribute on the left side:
    \begin{equation*} 30 \cdot \frac{x}{3} + 30 \cdot \frac{2}{5} = 30 \cdot \frac{3}{10}. \end{equation*}
    Next, we cancel where applicable. In the first term (all content before the first plus sign), the \(30\) in the numerator partially cancels with the \(3\) in the denominator, or said differently, after cancelling factor of \(3\) on top and bottom, we are left with a factor of \(10\) on top. In the second term (all content between the plus sign and the equal sign), the \(30\) in the numerator partially cancels with the \(5\) in the denominator, leaving a factor of \(6\) on top. On the right side, the \(30\) in the numerator partially cancels with the \(10\) in the denominator, leaving a factor of \(3\) on top. Here’s what it looks like with the cancellations written in:
    \begin{equation*} \cancel{3} \cdot 10 \cdot \frac{x}{\cancel{3}} + \cancel{5} \cdot 6 \cdot \frac{2}{\cancel{5}} = \cancel{10} \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{3}{\cancel{10}}. \end{equation*}
    After performing the cancellations, we are left with:
    \begin{equation*} 10x + 12 = 9. \end{equation*}
    This is an equation with no fractions, so we can solve it using the strategies we have learned previously. Subtracting \(12\) from both sides gives:
    \begin{equation*} 10x = -3. \end{equation*}
    Dividing both sides by \(10\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} x = -\frac{3}{10}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.4.3.

    Before seeing more examples, let’s discuss why this strategy works:
    • We multiplied by the appropriate quantity (in our last example \(30\)) which ensured that, after distributing where needed, all factors in the denominator would cancel. Using the least common multiple of the denominators as the number to multiply by on both sides ended up leading to this value appearing as a factor in the numerator of each term, allowing each copy to create all the necessary cancellations so that each denominator ends up being \(1\) after cancellations occur. In other words, this ends up creating an equation without fractions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a fraction expression, there are not two sides, so to simplify adding fractions, a common denominator is needed. However, when you have an equation there are two sides. We can take avantage of the two sides by multiplying by the same thing on both sides! In the case of an expression, there aren’t “sides” in the first place.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In an equation with fractions, multiplying both sides by the least common multilple of the denominators is the fastest way to get rid of fractions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.4.

    Solve the equation \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac23 = \frac{3x}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We are being asked to solve an equation with fractions, so Strategy 1.4.1 applies. The least common multiple of the denominators \(6\) and \(3\) and \(4\) is \(12\text{.}\) So we multiply both sides of the equation by \(12\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 12 \left( \frac{x}{6} + \frac{2}{3} \right) = 12 \cdot \frac{3x}{4}. \end{equation*}
    Distributing on the left side gives:
    \begin{equation*} 12 \cdot \frac{x}{6} + 12 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 12 \cdot \frac{3x}{4}. \end{equation*}
    Now we cancel where applicable.
    \begin{equation*} \cancel{6} \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{x}{\cancel{6}} + \cancel{3} \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{2}{\cancel{3}} = \cancel{4} \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{3x}{\cancel{4}}. \end{equation*}
    After performing the cancellations, we are left with:
    \begin{equation*} 2x + 8 = 9x. \end{equation*}
    This is an equation with no fractions. Subtracting \(2x\) from both sides gives:
    \begin{equation*} 8 = 7x. \end{equation*}
    Dividing both sides by \(7\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{8}{7}=x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    You may have never heard of Strategy 1.4.1 before, but it is the fastest way to solve any equation that has fractions. You may be used to another method that works involving common denominators, but as a friendly reminder, Warning 1.2.56 told us that even when we looked at fractional expressions, we didn’t always need common denominators. Let’s compare the two methods in the next example.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.5.

    Solve \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac43\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Here’s what it looks like to solve \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3}\) by ignoring Strategy 1.4.1 and doing everything with common denominators instead:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5x}{30} + \frac{3(x-3)}{30} = \frac{40}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5x}{30} + \frac{3x-9}{30} = \frac{40}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5x + 3x - 9}{30} = \frac{40}{30} \end{equation*}
    Multiply both sides by \(30\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 3x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x = 49 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{49}{8} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Here’s what it looks like to solve \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3}\) by using Strategy 1.4.1. First, the least common multiple of the denominators \(6\) and \(10\) and \(3\) is \(30\text{,}\) so we make our first step to multiply both sides by \(30\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 30\left(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10}\right) = 30 \cdot \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    On the left side, distribute \(30\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 30 \frac{x}{6} + 30 \frac{x-3}{10} = 30 \cdot \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    After cancelling, we have:
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 3(x-3) = 10 \cdot 4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 3x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x = 49 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{49}{8} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s be clear that both answers to the previous question are completely valid. In both answers, it took multiplying by \(30\) to get rid of the fractions. (I ask you to pause for a second and think about this: if we didn’t multiply by \(30\text{,}\) we would not have been able to get rid of the fractions.) Since we have to multiply by \(30\) anyway, why not do it right away and save ourselves some time? This saves us time because we don’t have to keep writing fractions. Fractions take longer to write than non-fractions (because fractions have a top and a bottom). To be clear, there are ways to skip some of the steps in both answers above (but for a fair comparison, both answers stated the original equation at the start of the answer).
    🔗
    Strategy 1.4.1 works because an equation has two sides. If we only had an expression, then we don’t have two “sides” to work with.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.6.

    Simplify the expression \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{5x}{30} + \frac{3(x-3)}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{5x}{30} + \frac{3x-9}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{5x+3x-9}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{8x-9}{30} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In the previous example, we could not use Strategy 1.4.1 because there is no second “side” to multiply by the least common multiple. Instead, we had to find a common denominator in order to simplify the addition of fractions. This is why Strategy 1.4.1 only applies to equations with fractions. However, when we have an equation with fractions, even if there is a plus sign between two fractions, there is no requirement to simplify the addition of fractions. Instead, we can use Strategy 1.4.1 to get rid of the fractions entirely.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.7.

    Solve the equation \(\frac{2x}{9} + \frac{3x+1}{10} = \frac{5x}{6}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    An answer that ignores (((xref without ref, first/last, or provisional attribute (check spelling)))) and uses common denominators instead looks like this:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x}{9} + \frac{3x+1}{10} = \frac{5x}{6} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{20x}{90} + \frac{9(3x+1)}{90} = \frac{75x}{90} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{47x+9}{90} = \frac{75x}{90} \end{equation*}
    Multiply both sides by \(90\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 47x + 9 = 75x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} -28x = -9 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{9}{28} \end{equation*}
    To try to speed things up, we skipped writing several steps.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    An answer that uses (((xref without ref, first/last, or provisional attribute (check spelling)))) first computes that the least common multiple of \(9\) and \(10\) and \(6\) is \(90\text{.}\) To create a fair comparison with the previous answer, we start by writing the original equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x}{9} + \frac{3x+1}{10} = \frac{5x}{6} \end{equation*}
    Now, if we were in the setting of a quiz or exam, this equation is probably already typed for you. For the multiplying by \(90\) on both sides, you could actually handwrite the \(90\) on both sides, surrounding the typed left side in parentheses. That is, by handwriting on top of the original equation, you could see this next equation without having to write it in full:
    \begin{equation*} 90 \left(\frac{2x}{9} + \frac{3x+1}{10}\right) = 90 \cdot \frac{5x}{6} \end{equation*}
    In fact, the step that would appear after this involves distributing \(90\) on the left, as we saw in previous examples using (((xref without ref, first/last, or provisional attribute (check spelling)))). Instead of writing the equation
    \begin{equation*} 90 \cdot \frac{2x}{9} + 90 \cdot \frac{3x+1}{10} = 90 \cdot \frac{5x}{6} \end{equation*}
    we might just stare at each term to figure out what results after cancellation. In other words, by looking at \(\frac{2x}{9}\) we could see that result of multiplying by \(90\) is \(10 \cdot 2x\text{.}\) Similarly, looking at the second term \(\frac{3x+1}{10}\text{,}\) multiplying by \(90\) gives \(9(3x+1)\text{.}\) For the term on the right side, multiplying \(\frac{5x}{6}\) by \(90\) simplifies to \(15 \cdot 5x\text{.}\) Thus, our first full equation to write could be this equation, which has no fractions:
    \begin{equation*} 20x + 9(3x+1) = 75x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 20x + 27x + 9 = 75x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 47x + 9 = 75x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} -28x = -9 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{9}{28} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.8.

    Solve \(\frac{2x+1}{3} - \frac{x-1}{4} = \frac{x+2}{6}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    An answer that ignores (((xref without ref, first/last, or provisional attribute (check spelling)))) and uses common denominators looks like this:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{8(2x+1)}{24} - \frac{6(x-1)}{24} = \frac{4(x+2)}{24} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{16x+8 - 6x + 6}{24} = \frac{4x + 8}{24} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{10x + 14}{24} = \frac{4x + 8}{24} \end{equation*}
    Multiply both sides by \(24\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 10x + 14 = 4x + 8 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 6x = -6 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = -1 \end{equation*}
    To try to minimize the amount of writing, some steps were skipped. Even with skipped steps, there were three equations written after the original equation which had fractions, and fractions take longer to write than non-fractions. (One common point of confusion is why the left side of the second equation has \(16x+8-6x+6\) instead of \(16x+8-6x-6\text{.}\) The reason for this is that the numerator on the left is really \(8(2x+1)-6(x-1)\) before any distributing occured. Some people would prefer to distribute before combining fractions, in which case one numerator is \(16x+8\) in a hidden set of parentheses and the second numerator is \((6x-6)\) also in a hidden set of parentheses.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Using (((xref without ref, first/last, or provisional attribute (check spelling)))), the least common multiple of \(3\) and \(4\) and \(6\) is \(12\text{.}\) We could handwrite a \(12\) on each side of the original typed equation, surrounding the left side in parentheses. Knowing that we will distribute \(12\) on the left side anyway, we can really just think of what it means to multiply each term on each side by \(12\text{.}\) Multiplying \(\frac{2x+1}{3}\) by \(12\) means that a factor of \(4\) remains in the numerator from the \(12\) so we’d have \(4(2x+1)\text{.}\) Multiplying \(\frac{x-1}{4}\) by \(12\) means that a factor of \(3\) remains in the numerator from the \(12\) so we’d have \(3(x-1)\) after the minus sign. Multiplying \(\frac{x+2}{6}\) by \(12\) means that a factor of \(2\) remains in the numerator from the \(12\) so we’d have \(2(x+2)\text{.}\) So, the first full equation we write doesn’t have fractions:
    \begin{equation*} 4(2x+1)-3(x-1)=2(x+2) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x+4-3x+3=2x+4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 5x+7=2x+4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 3x=-3 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x=-1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In an attempt to make a fair comparison and skip as much as people might eventually feel comfortable in writing (just to compare how much writing there is, and how much time it takes), a certain aspect of the first answer may have been confusing. For that reason, outside of the comparison, I think it would be good for us to see the first several steps of the first answer without skipping any work. There are actually two subvariants of the first answer, and we’ll go through the steps of both subvariants. Spending the time to do this isn’t to persuade you to move away from Strategy 1.4.1. Far from it. In fact, the subtle things that we’re about to dig into aren’t a problem by following Strategy 1.4.1. That said, going into the details (as annoying as it might be) I hope will demystify some of the steps that are too often glossed over quickly (and I don’t blame people who are confused)! The throughout explanations we’re about to dig into are meant to ensure we don’t create confusion by shoving things under the rug.
    • Starting from \(\frac{2x+1}{3} - \frac{x-1}{4} = \frac{x+2}{6}\) the usual work to create a common denominator leads to
      \begin{equation*} \frac{8(2x+1)}{24} - \frac{6(x-1)}{24} = \frac{4(x+2)}{24}. \end{equation*}
      At this moment, we can either distribute \(8\) and distribute \(6\text{,}\) or since we have common denominators on the left, we can combine the fractions. I don’t think it’s wise at this moment to try to do both at the same time. One thing at a time. So, let’s see what happens if we rewrite each denominator by distributing:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{16x+8}{24} - \frac{6x-6}{24} = \frac{4x+8}{24} \end{equation*}
      Now, before doing the next step, something that tiny that can get easily ignored can lead to problems. Recall that the entire numerator and entire denominator of every fraction is in a set of parentheses. For this problem, we need to really think about this for the numerators (and don’t need to really think about it for the denominators). While we really actually need to think about these parentheses for the second numerator, let’s just draw in thoe hidden parentheses for all numerators, making visible those once-hidden parentheses:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{(16x+8)}{24} - \frac{(6x-6)}{24} = \frac{(4x+8)}{24} \end{equation*}
      Now, on the left side, since we are subtracting fractions of a common denominator, we can combine this into one fraction: we leave in the parentheses for now, just to process one thing at a time.
      \begin{equation*} \frac{(16x+8)-(6x-6)}{24} = \frac{(4x+8)}{24} \end{equation*}
      The first set of parentheses can be dropped. The parentheses on the right side can also be dropped. However, the second set of parentheses on the left side cannot be dropped because of the minus sign in front of it. In fact, we addressed the expression \((16x+8)-(6x-6)\) in Example 1.1.69, so we can apply what we learned there:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{16x+8-6x+6}{24} = \frac{4x+8}{24} \end{equation*}
      We’ll stop working on the problem here, because the previous equation appeared in the first answer we already provided.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Starting over, once we got common denominators which gave the equation
      \begin{equation*} \frac{8(2x+1)}{24} - \frac{6(x-1)}{24} = \frac{4(x+2)}{24}, \end{equation*}
      instead of distributing first, we could have combined the fractions first:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{8(2x+1)-6(x-1)}{24} = \frac{4(x+2)}{24}, \end{equation*}
      Now, distributing \(8\) and \(-6\) and \(4\) gives us the equation:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{16x+8-6x+6}{24} = \frac{4x+8}{24}, \end{equation*}
      Again, we stop working on the problem here.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Clarifications, Cautions, and Connections

    Warning 1.4.9.

    When being asked to solve an equation with fractions, a common error occurs among those who insist on a common denominator approach. What usually happens is that there is adding and/or subtracting on both sides to reach the goal of having one side of the equation becoming just zero. After achieving this, often the work shown will involve only looking at the busier side and simplifying the expression.
    🔗
    Let’s illustrate exactly what this looks like with an example. When asked to solve \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3}\text{,}\) someone might first subtract \(\frac43\text{,}\) and their work in full might look like this:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} - \frac{4}{3} = 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =\frac{5x}{30} + \frac{3x-9}{30} - \frac{40}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =\frac{5x+3x-9-40}{30} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =\frac{8x-49}{30} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Doesn’t the end of this work seem a little strange? Normally, when we solve an equation, we expect the last line of work to look something like \(x=19\) or perhaps \(x=\frac{31}{7}\text{.}\) The end of the work didn’t tell us what number \(x\) should be. Instead, our final work was an expression. How did this happen? Often, when people see a zero on one side (as you see in the work above), people feel like zero is insignificant, and feel no need to keep copying this from one line to the next. This mistake does not happen when using Strategy 1.4.1, which gives another compelling reason (besides speed) to use that strategy. The mistake made earlier never seems to happen in an equation without fractions, but just to demonstrate and to intentionally try to create that error, if someone is asked to solve \(5x+6 = 3x -10\) and someone shows as their steps
    \begin{equation*} 5x+6 = 3x-10 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 5x+6 - 3x + 10 = 0 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =2x+16 \end{equation*}
    and declares that their final answer is \(2x+16\text{,}\) that seems strange.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.10.

    Solve \(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10} = \frac{4}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    After multiplying both sides by \(30\text{,}\) effectively, we can think about what cancellations are created by multiplying each term by \(30\text{.}\) The new first term would be \(5x\text{,}\) the new second term would be \(3(x-3)\text{,}\) and the new third term would be \(10 \cdot 4\text{.}\) So, we have:
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 3(x-3) = 10 \cdot 4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 3x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x - 9 = 40 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x = 49 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{49}{8} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Just to be thorough, the steps that we didn’t show were right at the beginning, namely,
    \begin{equation*} 30\left(\frac{x}{6} + \frac{x-3}{10}\right) = 30 \cdot \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 30 \cdot \frac{x}{6} + 30 \cdot \frac{x-3}{10} = 30 \cdot \frac{4}{3} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.4.11.

    Solve \(\frac{2x+1}{3} - \frac{x-1}{4} = \frac{x+2}{6} + \frac{x}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The least common multiple of \(3\) and \(4\) and \(6\) and \(3\) is \(12\text{.}\) Multiplying both sides by \(12\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} 12\left(\frac{2x+1}{3} - \frac{x-1}{4}\right) = 12\left(\frac{x+2}{6} + \frac{x}{3}\right) \end{equation*}
    Distributing on both sides gives:
    \begin{equation*} 12 \cdot \frac{2x+1}{3} - 12 \cdot \frac{x-1}{4} = 12 \cdot \frac{x+2}{6} + 12 \cdot \frac{x}{3} \end{equation*}
    After cancelling, we have:
    \begin{equation*} 4(2x+1) - 3(x-1) = 2(x+2) + 4x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 8x + 4 - 3x + 3 = 2x + 4 + 4x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 5x + 7 = 6x + 4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 3=x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The original equation was slightly different from our earlier examples in that the original equation had multiple fractions on each side. However, Strategy 1.4.1 still applies: the strategy applies to any equation with fractions, no matter how many fractions are on each side.
    🔗

    Expectation: equations where the variable appears in the denominator.

    If the original equation had variables in the denominator, check to ensure there are no false solutions. (Any solution which causes division by zero to occur is a false solution.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.12.

    Solve \(\frac{3}{2x-4} - \frac5{x+3} = \frac2{x-2}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Because the equation we are asked to solve has fractions, we use Strategy 1.4.1. It will be easier to compute the least common multiple of the denominators if we factor the first denominator: \(2x-4=2(x-2)\text{.}\) The least common multiple of \(2(x-2)\) and \(x+3\) and \(x-2\) is \(2(x-2)(x+3)\text{.}\) So we will multiply both sides by \(2(x-2)(x+3)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 2(x-2)(x+3) \left( \frac{3}{2(x-2)} - \frac5{x+3} \right) = 2(x-2)(x+3) \cdot \frac2{x-2} \end{equation*}
    Just a friendly reminder: the original left side had two terms, so due to Order of Operations, to indicate that we are multiplying the entire left side, we wrapped the original left side in parentheses. Now we distribute \(2(x-2)(x+3)\text{.}\) The language being used here can be confusing: I don’t mean that we look at \(2(x-2)(x+3)\) and expand this in any way, which is not only valid, this is a completely reasonable interpretation of distributing in this context. I mean that we place a copy of \(2(x-2)(x+3)\) to the side of each of the original terms on the left.
    \begin{equation*} 2(x-2)(x+3) \cdot \frac{3}{2(x-2)} - 2(x-2)(x+3) \cdot \frac5{x+3} = 2(x-2)(x+3) \cdot \frac2{x-2} \end{equation*}
    In the first term (which is everything before the minus sign), a factor of \(2\) and a factor of \((x-2)\) cancel on top and bottom. In the second term, a factor of \((x+3)\) cancels. On the right side, a factor of \((x-2)\) cancels. We are left with
    \begin{equation*} (x+3) \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 5 (x-2) = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot (x+3) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 3(x+3) -10(x-2) = 4(x+3) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 3x+9 - 10x + 20 = 4x + 12 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} -7x + 29 = 4x + 12 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 17 = 11x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{17}{11} = x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since the original equation had the variable written in the denominator, Summary  applies, and we need to check that the solution that we got doesn’t cause any denominator to be zero.
    • The first denominator is \(2x-4\text{.}\) When we put in \(x=\frac{17}{11}\text{,}\) we see \(2 \cdot \frac{17}{11} - 4 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When we put in \(x=\frac{17}{11}\) into the second denominator \(x+3\text{,}\) we see \(\frac{17}{11} + 3 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When we put in \(x=\frac{17}{11}\) into the third denominator \(x-2\text{,}\) we see \(\frac{17}{11} - 2 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    We checked that \(x=\frac{17}{11}\) does not cause any of the three denominators to simplify to zero. Therefore, \(x=\frac{17}{11}\) is a solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.13.

    Solve \(\frac{3}{x+1} - \frac12 = \frac1{3x+3}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The third denominator in factored form is \(3(x+1)\text{.}\) The least common multiple of \(x+1\) and \(2\) and \(3(x+1)\) is \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot (x+1)\text{,}\) or in other words, \(6(x+1)\text{.}\) We multiply both sides by \(6(x+1)\text{,}\) noting that we have to wrap the left side in parentheses:
    \begin{equation*} 6(x+1) \left(\frac{3}{x+1} - \frac12\right) = 6(x+1) \cdot \frac1{3(x+1)} \end{equation*}
    Now we distribute \(6(x+1)\text{.}\) By this, I’m not saying that we write \(6x+6\text{.}\) Instead, I mean that a copy of \(6(x+1)\) is written to the side of both fractinos on the left:
    \begin{equation*} 6(x+1) \cdot \frac{3}{x+1} - 6(x+1) \cdot \frac12 = 6(x+1) \cdot \frac1{3(x+1)} \end{equation*}
    After processing cancellations,
    \begin{equation*} 18 - 3(x+1) = 2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 18 - 3x - 3 = 2 \end{equation*}
    Now, there are multiple ways to proceed, so don’t fret if your next steps look different from my next steps:
    \begin{equation*} 15 -3x = 2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 12 = 3x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 4 = x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since the original equation had the variable written in the denominator, Summary  applies, and we need to check that the solution that we got doesn’t cause any denominator to be zero.
    • When we put in \(x=4\) into the first denominator \(x+1\text{,}\) we see \(4+1 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The second denominator is \(2\) without needing to substitute for \(x\text{,}\) since there was no \(x\) in the second denominator. Note \(2 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • When we put in \(x=4\) into the third denominator \(3x+3\text{,}\) we see \(3 \cdot 4 + 3 = 15 \not= 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    We checked that \(x=4\) does not cause any of the three denominators to become zero. Therefore, \(x=4\) is a solution.

    Note 1.4.15.

    At this point, we have two different concepts of checking an equation. To be transparent with you, it’s time for some clarification. Let’s compare:
    • In Summary , we gave an expectation, or really a requirement: after solving an equation where the variable appears in the denominator, we are required to check that the value(s) obtanied for \(x\) do not cause any denominator to equal zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In Principle 1.3.39, we mentioned that you can always see if the value(s) of \(x\) make the left and right side of the original equation to be equal. This check is never required, but it is recommended. If we encounter a time when the left and right side do not end up being equal, we should scan our work for any errors. After all, we’re all human!
      🔗
      🔗
    The purpose of discussing this is to spell out that the two checks are checking for different things. In the first case, we’re just checking to see that denominators are never zero, and this check is required whenever the variable appears in the denominator. In the second, we’re checking for something else (do the left and right sides of the original equation equal each other), and only because getting different values for the left and right hints to us that there’s an error in our work, and we should go over it. In fact, we only completed the first kind of check in Example 1.4.12, and we didn’t do the second kind of check mentioned in Principle 1.3.39. To illustrate that point more fully, we can check that putting in \(\frac{17}{11}\) in for \(x\) into both the left side \(\frac{3}{2x-4}-\frac{5}{x+3}\) and the right side \(\frac{2}{x-2}\) shows that both sides equal \(\frac{22}{-5}\) or from a calculator, \(-4.4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In summary, when the variable appears in the denominator, we must check that we never have zero in the denominator due to the value(s) of \(x\text{.}\) In any equation, we can always check to see if the original left and right sides are equal for the sake of quality control.
